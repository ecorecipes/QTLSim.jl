Quantitative genetics of a changing environment

Limits to the rate of adaptation to a steadily changing environment

Pagel, Gardner and Meade (2025) simulated 119,984 populations adapting to a moving optimum and concluded that populations can sustain change of only a few percent of a phenotypic standard deviation per generation. Here we work through the model, its results and the assumptions on which they depend.

Background

Many natural populations are changing in response to a warming climate, and phenotypic traits such as the timing of breeding have shifted measurably over a few decades. Whether such change can continue depends on whether evolution can keep pace with the environment. While plasticity allows individuals to respond within their lifetime, sustained change over many generations requires a genetic response, and the rate of that response is limited by the genetic variance on which selection acts.

Quantitative-genetic theory has long addressed this question for a trait under stabilising selection toward an optimum that moves at a constant rate (Lynch and Lande 1993; Bürger and Lynch 1995). A population follows the optimum with a lag, and the lag reduces mean fitness; above a critical rate of change, the lag cannot be sustained and the population declines to extinction. The critical rate depends on the genetic variance, the strength of selection and the population's capacity for growth, all of which are difficult to measure in the field.

Pagel, Gardner and Meade (Scientific Reports, 2025) approached the problem by simulation. Each of 119,984 populations, with its own genetic architecture and strength of selection, was allowed to reach equilibrium under stabilising selection, after which its optimum moved by a fixed amount every generation. The populations that persisted changed at a median of 0.031 phenotypic standard deviations per generation, and the authors concluded that rates of change already observed in some natural populations exceed what most of their simulated populations could sustain.

The model

Genetic variance at equilibrium

Each population consists of 1000 diploid individuals with a single trait, whose value is the sum of the allelic effects at 100 loci plus an environmental deviation. Populations start without genetic variation. Mutations, each adding a small random effect at a single locus, introduce variance, which accumulates over several thousand generations until it is balanced by its loss to selection and drift.

Stabilising selection

Fitness declines with distance from an optimum according to a Gaussian function, and each individual survives with a probability equal to its fitness. Survivors mate at random, and each offspring receives one gamete from each of two parents. At equilibrium the genetic variance accounts for a fixed proportion of the phenotypic variance, here 60%, and the population is distributed around the optimum.

A moving optimum

At generation 10,000 the optimum starts to move by a fixed amount every generation, here 0.008. Selection now favours individuals ahead of the mean, and the mean phenotype responds in proportion to the genetic variance. The population therefore follows the optimum with a lag, which settles at a value at which the response to selection matches the rate of change.

Extinction

If the optimum moves faster, here at 0.015 per generation, the response to selection cannot match it. The lag grows, fewer individuals survive selection each generation, and the population goes extinct 954 generations after the optimum started to move. Because the population is restored to its full size each generation, extinction occurs only when selection removes nearly every individual.

The lag

Measured in phenotypic standard deviations, the lag of the slower population fluctuates around a constant, whereas that of the faster population increases without bound. Whether a population persists therefore depends on whether a stable lag exists at the rate at which the environment changes.

How fast is too fast

Persistence

Across all simulated populations, persistence depends primarily on the rate of environmental change expressed in phenotypic standard deviations per generation, x. Half of the populations persist for 2000 generations at about 0.07 standard deviations per generation, and almost none persist above 0.2. The grey line shows all of the published populations; the coloured lines show a stratified sample of 710 of them, in the original simulations and when simulated again with QTLSim.

Time to extinction

Above the threshold, the time to extinction declines steeply with the rate of change, from more than a thousand generations at 0.1 standard deviations per generation to about a hundred at 0.5. Consequently, populations exposed to rapid change may be lost within a few decades for organisms with short generation times.

The lag

The lag increases in proportion to the rate of change, as expected when the response to selection is proportional to the distance from the optimum.

Mortality

Mortality rises with the lag, from almost none at lags of a few hundredths of a standard deviation to about half at a lag of 0.2 and nearly all individuals at a lag of 1. The lag therefore links the rate of environmental change to the demographic cost of selection.

The sustainable rate

Of the 119,984 simulated populations, 6158 persisted for 240,000 generations after the optimum started to move. These populations changed at a median of 0.031 phenotypic standard deviations per generation, which the authors took as an estimate of the maximum rate of change that populations can sustain. As populations with larger mutational input were more likely to persist, the authors also reweighted the survivors to the distribution of mutational input across all populations, which reduced the estimate to 0.019.

The authors then calculated, for each surviving population, the critical rate of Bürger and Lynch (1995), which depends on its genetic variance, the strength of selection and the number of surviving parents. Birds advance their breeding by a median of 0.022 phenotypic standard deviations per year (Radchuk et al. 2019); with generation times of two to four years this corresponds to 0.044 to 0.088 per generation, at the 68th and 86th percentiles of the critical rates. If these rates reflect a genetic response, they suggest that many natural populations may already be changing faster than they could sustain.

What sets the limit

Genetic variance

The response to selection, and hence the sustainable rate, is set by the genetic variance present when the environment begins to change. In the simulations this variance is maintained by a balance between mutation, selection and drift, and it is close to the prediction of Bürger's stochastic house-of-cards approximation (median ratio 0.89). Populations with weak mutational input carry less variance, and the persisting populations had larger mutational input than the populations as a whole.

Linkage

In the simulation program, each gamete has at most one crossover, so that loci on the same chromosome are inherited together more often than they would be with free recombination. Linkage between loci under selection reduces the genetic variance available to selection. When every crossover point is applied, populations hold 1.06 times as much genetic variance and persist 1.12 times as long, suggesting that the published estimates are somewhat conservative with respect to recombination.

Demography

The simulations keep population size constant, so that a poorly adapted population is restored to its full size each generation. In natural populations, by contrast, maladaptation reduces population size, which in turn reduces the number of individuals on which selection can act. When the same populations are allowed to decline, with density-dependent growth of the Beverton–Holt form and maximum growth rates of 5, 2 and 1.5 offspring per individual, the proportion persisting for 2000 generations falls from 8.8% to 6.7%, 4.4% and 3.4%.

A lower sustainable rate

The rate of change at which half of the populations persist falls from 0.077 standard deviations per generation at constant size to between 0.056 and 0.065, and populations that go extinct do so in 0.37 to 0.60 of the time. A model of constant size may therefore overestimate the rate of change that populations can sustain, particularly for species with low reproductive capacity.

Caveats

The model describes a single trait under Gaussian stabilising selection, in a population of fixed size, with an optimum that moves at a constant rate. Natural environments fluctuate around their trends, and fluctuations are known to lower the rate of change that a population can sustain (Bürger and Lynch 1995). Conversely, many of the changes observed in the field, including shifts in breeding time, are at least partly plastic, and plasticity allows a population to track its environment without a genetic response. Comparisons between simulated and observed rates also depend on generation times and on how rates measured over different intervals are standardised. These considerations do not alter the qualitative conclusion that the sustainable rate is limited by the genetic variance and the demographic cost of selection, but they affect where any particular population lies relative to that limit.

Methods

The published populations fall into eight strata of about 15,000, defined by the number of loci and the mean number of crossover points per gamete. Within each stratum, every population drew its own phenotypic variance, heritability, mutational input, strength of selection and rate of change. Each population was simulated for 10,000 generations under stabilising selection, after which the optimum moved until the population went extinct or 240,000 generations had passed.

Parameters across all published populations: 5th, 50th and 95th percentiles

The summary statistics reported in the paper were recomputed from its table of results for each population. All of them are reproduced, including the adjustment for mutational input, which uses the maximum-likelihood gamma distribution fitted to the mutation scalars of all populations, and the critical rate, which follows Bürger and Lynch (1995) with a factor of 2 and the variance Vλ = Vs + σg² + Vs/2Ne in place of the Vs + σg² of the printed equation.

Summary statistics as reported in the paper and recomputed from its table of results (median, quartiles)

We then simulated a stratified random sample of 710 of the published populations with QTLSim.jl, an independent implementation of the model, using the published parameters and different random numbers; each simulation stopped 2000 generations after the optimum started to move, and those still alive were continued to 240,000 generations. Compared with the original simulations of the same populations, the genetic variance before the move differed by a factor of 0.990 (95% interval 0.973–1.007) and the time to extinction by 0.999 (0.992–1.006). Of the 41 populations that persisted in the original simulations, 40 persisted again, and no population persisted only in the new simulations.
