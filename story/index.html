A reproduction, step by step

How fast can a population adapt to a changing environment?

Pagel, Gardner and Meade simulated 119,984 populations to find out. We recomputed their numbers, reran 710 of their populations with an independent implementation, and asked what changes the answer.

The claim

As climates warm, the traits that suit a population best shift: the time to breed, the size to grow, the temperature to tolerate. A population can follow by evolving, but only so fast. How fast is too fast?

Pagel, Gardner and Meade (Scientific Reports, 2025) answered with a simulation, QTSim. Each of 119,984 virtual populations spent 10,000 generations adapting to a fixed optimum; then the optimum started to move, a fixed amount every generation, until the population died out or 240,000 generations had passed. Only 6158 survived. Those survivors had changed at a median of 0.031 phenotypic standard deviations per generation: the paper's estimate of the maximum sustainable rate of change, about 3% of a standard deviation per generation.

The paper then compared field data with a theoretical critical rate for each surviving population. Birds advance their breeding by a median of 0.022 standard deviations a year, which at two to four years per generation is 0.044 to 0.088 per generation: at the 68th and 86th percentiles of the survivors' critical rates. Many populations, the paper concluded, may already be changing faster than they can sustain.

Here we walk through that analysis with QTLSim.jl, an independent Julia implementation of the same model: how it works, how the paper's numbers arise, whether they come out again, and which of the model's assumptions they depend on.

The model

A population of individuals

A population has 1000 diploid individuals. Each carries a trait, its phenotype: the sum of the effects of its alleles at 100 loci, its genotype, plus random environmental noise. In this example the phenotypic variance is 0.02 and 60% of it is genetic at equilibrium.

Every population starts with no genetic variation. Mutations, each adding a small random effect at a random locus, build it up over thousands of generations.

Selection toward an optimum

Fitness falls off on either side of an optimum like a bell curve. Each generation an individual survives with probability equal to its fitness; survivors pair at random and each offspring gets one gamete from each of two different parents, its chromosomes recombined at a random number of crossover points.

Mutation adds variance, selection and random drift remove it. After 10,000 generations the genetic variance has settled where the two balance.

The optimum starts to move

At generation 10,000 the optimum moves by a fixed amount d every generation. Here it moves by 0.008 per generation. Selection now favours individuals ahead of the mean, and the population follows, a little behind: it settles into a steady lag.

Too fast to follow

The same population, with the optimum moving at 0.015 per generation. The response cannot keep up: the lag grows, fewer individuals survive selection, and the population dies out 954 generations after the move.

What the paper measures

From each run the paper records how long the population lasted after the move, how fast its mean phenotype changed, and how far it lagged, in units of its phenotypic standard deviation at the moment half the population died in a generation. The rate of environmental change in those units, x, is what decides survival, as we will see.

The design

The paper's populations come in eight groups of about 15,000: 100, 500, 1000 or 5000 loci, with a mean of three or five crossover points per gamete. Within each group, every population drew its own phenotypic variance, heritability, mutation scalar, fitness width and rate of change.

The drawn parameters across all populations: 5th, 50th and 95th percentiles

The paper's numbers, recomputed

The paper's per-population results table comes with its code. Recomputing every summary the paper reports from that table pins down exactly how each number is defined, and they all come out again.

The paper's Table 2 and its survivors' statistics: as printed, and recomputed from its results table (median, quartiles)

Two of them needed some detective work. The adjustment for the mutation scalar weights each survivor by the density of its mutation scalar under a gamma distribution, which turns out to be the maximum-likelihood fit to all populations' scalars. And the critical rate in the table is Bürger and Lynch's formula: compared with the equation as printed in the paper, it has a factor of 2 and the variance Vλ = Vs + σg² + Vs/2Ne in place of Vs + σg². With those, the table's columns are reproduced exactly.

Rerunning 710 populations

A second, independent run

Recomputing the table checks the arithmetic, not the simulations. So we drew 710 of the paper's populations, from every group, and ran each again with QTLSim, from the same parameters but with different random numbers: a new realisation of the same population, not a copy of its run. Each rerun stops 2000 generations after the move, unless the population is still alive.

The share still alive at that point falls steeply with the standardised rate of change x. For all the paper's populations (grey), the published runs of the 710 (blue) and their reruns (orange), half are alive at about 0.07 standard deviations per generation, and almost none above 0.2.

What moves the answer

One crossover per gamete

Reproducing a program exactly means reproducing its quirks. QTSim draws several crossover points for each gamete but switches strand only at the lowest: every gamete has at most one crossover. With that behaviour, QTLSim's runs of 589 published inputs match the published runs (time to extinction ×1.008, genetic variance ×1.007). Applying every crossover point instead, populations live ×1.12 as long and hold ×1.06 the genetic variance: the published results depend on the quirk.

A population that can shrink

QTSim refills its population to 1000 every generation, however many survive selection. Real populations shrink when they are poorly adapted. Rerunning the same 710 populations with density-regulated growth (Beverton–Holt, with maximum growth rates R of 5, 2 and 1.5 offspring per individual), the share alive 2000 generations after the move falls from 8.8% to 6.7%, 4.4% and 3.4%.

A lower sustainable rate

The rate of change at which half the populations are still alive after 2000 generations falls from 0.077 standard deviations per generation at a fixed size to 0.065, 0.056 and 0.057, and populations that die do so in 0.60, 0.44 and 0.37 of the time. A fixed population size makes the sustainable rate look higher than it would be for a population that can decline.

Sources and methods

Everything here is computed by story/build_data.jl in QTLSim.jl from the runs stored with the package's vignettes: replicating Pagel, Gardner and Meade (the recomputation, the 710 reruns and the survivors), demography in the paper's populations (density regulation), and the comparison of crossover models with the published runs (validate/published_sweep.jl). The paper's own numbers come from its results tables, which come with its code and data, not with this package; only their summaries are shown here.
